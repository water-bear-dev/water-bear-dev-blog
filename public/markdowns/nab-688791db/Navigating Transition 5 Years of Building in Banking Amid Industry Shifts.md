Yesterday’s headlines announced significant workforce reductions across corporate and enabling functions at NAB—up to 2,500 roles across the organisation, with 1,500 slated before Christmas. Everyone was blindsided.

As someone who has spent the last five years here, and never had been impacted before, reading announcements like this is quite unexpected. From what I know, my workplace attracts tech talent quite heavily, not only in Australia, but in other countries as well. While consultation unfolds and many of us navigate uncertainty about whether our roles will be directly impacted, it feels like the right moment to step back and reflect: on what I built, how enterprise engineering actually works beneath the headlines, and where we go from here.

## The Enterprise Macro: Beyond the Headlines

When financial institutions announce large restructuring programs aimed at "simplifying operating models" and "reducing duplication," outside commentary often scrambles to point fingers—either blaming macroeconomic belt-tightening or framing it as wholesale AI replacement.

From my view, the reality is more nuanced:

- **The Cost of Corporate Layering:** Over multi-year transformation cycles, enterprise organisations naturally accumulate layers of coordination, project management, and cross-functional overhead. Periodic reorganisations are institutional course-corrections to flatten decision loops and cut dependency chains.
- **The Shift from AI Hype to Operational Efficiency:** Enterprise technology is past the era of running proof-of-concepts, saying AI is a 'buzzword' and frame it as an innovation. Today’s mandate is ruthless prioritisation: internal developer tooling, self-service platforms, and governance that tangibly save time or reduce systemic risk.
- **Execution Over Strategy:** High-level strategic slide decks don't keep core banking infrastructure resilient. What moves the needle is whether technical systems are decoupled, well-instrumented, and maintainable by lean, high-ownership engineering teams.

## Five Years of Building: What We Actually Delivered

Five years in banking technology is long enough to see things come and go, but the engineering fundamentals remain constant.

Looking back across my time here, the work I am proudest of wasn't defined by the size of the budgets or the complexity of the architecture diagrams. It came down to practical utility:

- **Bridging Platform and Developer Ergonomics:** Designing and delivering data platforms and AI tooling that internal teams actually wanted to use. When you build context engineering libraries and agentic frameworks that your own engineering colleagues adopt to cut through internal support friction, that’s where software feels genuinely impactful.
- **Engineering in High-Stakes Environments:** Delivering generative systems, contact centre intelligence, and automated pipelines inside strict security, authentication, and regulatory boundaries forces you to grow up quickly as an engineer. I have been fortunate enough to work in one of the most customer-facing departments in the bank. Through there, and working with other teams and squads, you learn that reliability, data lineage, and auditability aren't afterthoughts—they are the core product.
- **The People in the Trenches:** The systems we delivered only worked because of the exceptional engineers, product partners, and analysts beside us. In an enterprise, technical problems are rarely just code problems; they are alignment problems. The camaraderie built while navigating production incidents, architectural reviews, and regulatory milestones is the most lasting takeaway from the past half-decade.

## Facing Uncertainty with Perspective

Restructuring is a stressful time. When you’ve poured years of craft into systems and teams, sitting in the ambiguity of whether your role is in scope is challenging.

Yet, technical resilience mirrors personal resilience:

- **Control What You Can Control:** Corporate reorganisations happen at altitudes far above individual contributors. What remains entirely yours is the craft you sharpened, the integrity of your code, and the relationships you cultivated.
- **Separating Role from Identity:** A corporate title or tenure at a single institution does not define an engineer's capability. The ability to decompose messy problems, architect dependable systems, and mentor colleagues translates anywhere. It is hard, especially when knowing that your usual role is impacted, and that uncertainty has prompted me to constantly upskill myself, branching out of my comfort zone and explore what is out there.
- **The role of Unions:** I believe the Finance Sector Union is very effective, to an extent. I saw that they have been successful in negotiating the Enterprise Agreements for the place I am working and also in other major banks. They have been acting as a supportive voice for everyone working within the banking and financial sectors. But with redundancies this big, I don't know how will they do. I just hope for the best for my colleagues.
- **Family, friend or someone to talk to:** Stressful times can be devastating, so another set of ears would help a lot. I feel like venting or talking to someone is one of the better options, than having to hold everything within you.

## What’s Ahead

The tech landscape is undergoing one of the most significant architectural shifts in decades. As platforms mature, the demand will only increase for engineers who can look past the hype, understand data sovereignty and security, and build practical, resilient systems.

To my colleagues navigating the same news: take pride in what we’ve built, support one another through the weeks ahead, and keep your sights firmly on the long horizon.

Onwards.
